Why does past experience often seem to possess a certain sense of superiority to the experience of the present? In short, maybe it is related to our underlying reliance on meaning to discern an emotional connection to the events in our life.

Like most of my commentary here, this is a purely speculative piece. There are a number of ongoing research efforts attempting to unravel the exact neural mechanisms underlying nostalgia. I will focus more on the experience of this feeling. It seems obvious why we look back at the high points in our lives with warmth and affection. Certainly those times were objectively better than whatever we may be doing with our lives right now. This doesn't seem to capture the full complexity though, as we (or at least I) have the tendency to achieve a similar distortion from experiences that I did not enjoy the first time. This topic first came to mind while I was discussing a prior year in medical school with one of my classmates. I distinctly remembered feeling very unhappy going through that stage of my life. So did he. The curriculum was detached from a meaningful sense of inquiry, and patients unwittingly became theoretical constructs without our notice. Returning to my own thoughts on the experience though, I didn't feel the same underlying discomfort I know I felt at the time. In fact, I have been developing an increasing sense of fondness for the period of time in question. This seems to be especially common in the medical field. Grueling training regimes feel pointless and brutal at the moment, but the stinging resentment quickly fades once the most recent hurdle is behind us. Why is that?

When I turn my mind to other periods of past despair, my experience is similar. Even the most unpleasant of experiences (within reason) are blunted now. The yearlong stretch of my life in which I prepared for and applied to medical school now seems like a fulfilling endeavor with my future success all but guaranteed. I assure you that was not my experience at the time. I was plagued by uncertainty and despair virtually every waking day of that year. No matter which memory of the past I turn to, I seem to have a better connection with it than I do with my life as it is right now. I have posed this scenario to other people, and they generally seem to agree. I have spent a lot of time thinking about this since that conversation, and I think an explanation lies in two parts. In order to understand the answer, we must know how information is processed and stored (i.e. right now), as well as how we access and relate to that information at a later date (i.e. remembrance).

Meaning itself is a blurry concept. For my discussion here, I will take for granted that our perceptions and beliefs are formed by some interaction of the networks of neurons that reside in our brain. This is not controversial, but the details right now are incredibly vague.  The neural networks comprising ourselves and our own organic intelligence (not to be confused with computer science's version of artificial intelligence), consist of a series of interconnected neurons that generally represent the outline of our own unique version of the universe. The events that have happened or are happening to us are folded into that neural network, with a relationship to the totality of our past experience. At the very least this is true in a physical sense - I am not certain of the functional impact on our perceived experience.

As we interact with the world through our basic set of senses, information is parceled, processed, and piped throughout the brain according to a complex set of rules and relationships. The significance of these events is uncertain though. When I sit here writing this article, I do not know what will happen to these words. What meaning does this set of thoughts have in relationship to my life as a whole? Perhaps in the future someone will read this, seek me out, and engage in a fantastic conversation. Perhaps someone will find some aspect of it deeply troubling and enlighten me as to the limits of my understanding. I find it difficult to predict right now. If I were to fast forward 10 years though, I would more-or-less know the exact impact of this writing. Very likely I won't even remember it, signaling that there was indeed minimal significance to me writing this right now. Any anxiety I feel at the moment will have a complete set of connections to the other events of my life, rendering my lived experience less important than the eventual outcome of this event in relationship to everything else I have lived through. Essentially, as I live this moment right now I am filled with open questions about what I am doing and how it will work out. In the future, when I look back at this moment, I will know how it worked out and the sum total meaning of this event will be prioritized in my mind over how I feel right now (if I can even remember that).

To bring this back to the idea of neural networks, my perception as I engage with the world in the present moment of time is poorly connected to the network as a whole. Through an unknown mechanism, I experience uncertainty and the constant influx of partially processed data less favorably than I do the recall of more fully processed and connected data available after the fact. Perhaps the further removed I am from an event in time, the more meaning I can glean from it. That certainly feels to be the case thus far.

Already, I am looking back at the application process to medical school, and the subsequent three years spent in classrooms and hospital wards, within a context that cannot be decoupled from the outcome of each of those events. Indeed, it would not make functional sense for me to do otherwise. No longer can I fully experience the pain of every medical school rejection as though it is important. The frustration I felt doing research my first summer is now inconsequential. The stark absence of serious instruction throughout my clinical rotations feels expected now, and I also know that I will learn most of the material regardless. When I look back at each event, I have superimposed a larger contextual meaning to each event based on subsequent information. This is a beautiful construct that helps us move forward in life, through failure, heartbreak, or loss.

This constant restructuring of data into a meaningful hierarchy does leave us painfully open to self-deceit though. Now that I am not confronted with the experience of being cast aside as a medical student day-in and day-out, will I fight as ardently to ensure the system actually improves? I certainly hope and plan to, but it is difficult. This phenomenon likely plays a large part in why medical training, and the medical field in general, is so rigid. How many times have we heard a physician (or even a student once they have successfully endured the abuse) say that the training system must remain the way it is? They have moved beyond the simple data collection phase of the event, and they are now deriving meaning out of it based on outcomes. The trap we can so easily fall into is to then become complacent about improving the overall system, simply because we found the eventual result meaningful. This faulty mode of thinking extends far beyond the medical field.

So what can be done about this? Unfortunately I do not have a complete set of answers to share, as our understanding of our own bias in perceiving and remembering information is not fully understood. It seems apparent that a starting point should be a simple acknowledgement of our underlying preference when we discuss or engage with items from our past. When reflecting on past events, both pleasant and unpleasant, try to remember exactly how you felt about it then - and why. Focusing on how you feel about it now is beneficial in some ways, but presents an understanding even more incomplete than understandings normally are. There is certainly a trade-off to be made between optimizing the experience in the present with an outcome-based reflection from the future. To do so requires effort, though, so that the latter does not unconsciously dominate.

Beyond advice for the reflection of past events, what can we do in the present in order to mitigate the anxiety provoking, mind-numbing lack of certainty or meaning in our present actions? This requires some embrace of uncertainty or for the imagination of possibilities. It is extraordinarily easy to live one's life moment-to-moment without ever really enjoying it, until it is a memory that is. There is no shortage of advice to live in the moment. Perhaps a practical method to achieve this is by understanding that the moment we live in is incomplete. It's part of a much larger collection that we won't even begin to understand until much later. For the time being, the meaning of each action we take is what we make of it. As I sit here writing this, I can focus less on the eventual outcome of this line of thinking and more on this moment as the completion of prior moments in time. What about the conversation I had that started this all? Isn't this moment the current culmination of that moment more than anything else? My default is not to look at it as such, but maybe it should be. The future is not here yet and meaning will be assigned for us in the past, but the present is wide-open for interpretation. Let's make the most of it.

As a final thought, I am far from certain of the validity of anything I've just written, but I am even less certain of the applicability of this entire thought process on exceptionally painful suffering or prolonged injustice. Perhaps for many there is some aspect of it that will remain true in the automated, biological sense. For others I am certain that no amount of time removed can remodel the experience in a meaningful way, nor should it. For those who do find themselves growing less outraged with time, do not forget how you felt in the moment if someone with the same experience reaches out to you for help.
