Any foray into this topic comes with an obligatory disclaimer. This is a difficult topic, and it is hard to find someone in a rigorous academic discipline that hasn’t formed an opinion on the subject. And that is for a good reason. Free will is an immensely personal concept. It can be very uncomfortable to chip away at one’s own sense of individuality or mental ownership. Conversely, it can be incredibly appealing to pursue an understanding of human choice in which any individual is no longer expressly responsible for the decisions they make; each consciousness becomes an enormously complex biophysical machine outputting the approximation of behavioral choice. I remain open to all possibilities, and I hope that I always will. And, though it is of very little consequence to my species or to the universe what I think on free will, I find the exercise of asking these questions is often rewarded with thoughts and perspectives that might not have otherwise occurred to me. The scenarios and thoughts I outline here are nothing more than the starting blocks in my own philosophical journey to understand who, or what, I am.

I think it is prudent to start with an acknowledgement that we have been given far too much baggage to carry on this topic by our predecessors. The idea of free will, and the related vagaries of personal souls, can be traced back most concretely to Plato and Aristotle in ancient Greece. Regardless of whether that was the first substantial human exploration into the idea of self-determinism, it wasn’t until the rise of Christianity that ideas on the topic rapidly began to explode in the West. First with Augustine then Thomas Aquinas, and eventually René Descartes, Immanuel Kant, Thomas Reid, and, contemporarily, C.A. Campbell. I do not think it is helpful to jump into the specifics of each of these thinkers now but suffice it to say they have muddled the waters. It is not even completely apparent that each of them is referring to the same underlying concept when discussing free will. Today we are left with nomenclature and partially formed concepts that link directly back to a time in which we had no meaningful idea of the function of the brain and still defended non-material souls as plausibly separate from the rest of the universe.

There is undoubtedly some insight to be gained from the thoughts of these previous writers, but not if it comes at the cost of our own confusion on the issue. For my simplified purpose here, I will mostly disregard everything that has been said before. The time will come to re-link these ideas with my own. Right now, though, we must focus on neurons, the central nervous system, our bodies, and the thousands of actions each of us takes every day.

While we are on the topic of confusion, we should also discuss our expectation of what free will, itself, would look like if we were to find it. Some have argued, and this seems to be the more prevalent understanding by the public, that free will would allow one to ‘freely’ decide one’s next course of action. My initial thought is that this sets such a ridiculous expectation when searching for evidence of free will, that it prevents us from ever even asking a coherent set of questions. Truly free exercise of will in this sense would decouple a sentient organism from the logical and consequential world in which we live. To an outside observer, they would make decisions from moment to moment that seem unrelated to their current physical state, or even to cultural or personal norms. It would be schizophrenic behavior without schizophrenia. If I were to ask a truly free individual whether they would prefer to walk uptown or downtown, they may very well turn to me and let me know that they have decided to move to Wyoming to begin preparing to relocate to the moon. While it may very much align with a long-held desire of theirs or be a truly new and original choice that they just decided, human intelligence for the most part is bounded by the realm of the eminently possible, the geographically adjacent, and the temporally relevant. It seems clear that this is not the type of free will that we possess. We are bound in space and time for most of our decisions, and the scope of decisions brought before us at any point in time is limited by the sum of all other physical occurrences prior to the moment of decision. Maybe a broader scope of free decision would allow us to obtain a higher level of enlightenment, but it would also certainly guarantee the human species does not exist long. Many pre-determined patterns of behavior need to be rapidly accessed by any living organism in order to thrive in an environment with survival competition.

Therefore, the more relevant question to ask seems to be this: Are we physically capable of controlling any of the series of decisions we make each day in a manner that is not predetermined, and in which we *could* have chosen differently? Even this hastily created formulation of the question is inadequate, specifically in the way that it asks if something that does not happen, that *will* not happen, could happen. As a natural consequence of the previously outlined expectation of what free will might look like, our past experience, current understanding, and the state of the society we find ourselves in, will play a role in our eventual decision. And by nature of the kind of organism we are, we can only make one decision at a time that can be physically acted upon. So, in that sense, it is very easy to say that we *couldn’t* choose differently because we chose in line with everything we knew at the time and could only have made one choice after it is all said and done. This is intriguing, but I think it’s a linguistic side-step around the problem we truly want to solve. Other animals certainly do embody this kind of understanding of free will. Their decisions are highly patterned and heavily reliant on external stimuli to elicit a response. Humans sometimes do this but our ability to self-reflect prevents me from going all the way to say we are the same.

So, to restate the question, can we exercise free will? Here’s where I am now: It depends and probably not, most of the time. Looking for an absolute answer, ‘yes we do’ or ‘no we do not’ is antiquated given what we know about the brain. From the perspective of moment-by-moment decision making, we are capable of a relevant recall of previous experience and internal deliberation on those memories, but within a narrow scope of how we understand those experiences, and even in the extent of the content of those experiences.

To put this in context, let’s imagine a common scenario. A friend has texted me about meeting up for lunch. Even as we begin this thought experiment, the scope of my individual choice has become narrowed by context. Because my friend has brought forward a proposition, I am now faced with responding to that specific external stimulus, and I don’t have much option about whether or not I can respond because after the text has come to my attention, my next action is inextricably linked to that external stimulus. Broadly speaking, I can choose to postpone, to ignore the friend, accept and offer a location, or accept and ask for a location. These are appropriate for the context and my specific society, and largely predetermined before I am confronted with the decision. I can also do something outside the scope of those options, like call and yell at my friend for no reason or drop my phone down the nearest grate in the street. The cost of making those decisions, though, is too high when I weigh them against the negative consequences.

Now I send a reply to my friend accepting and telling them which corner I will meet them on, actions that largely mirror my preset habits and that do not require internal reflection. It is unclear that I exercise any free will at this point.

Upon arriving at the café, I scan over a list of drinks, trying to decide which one looks good. The vast majority of times this is not an example of free will. I have a previous set of experiences in the world and have developed a set of preferences. For many people these preferences are also very rigid. Almost every single time I am in this scenario I will choose the same item, either water or coffee depending on a summation of events that have occurred that day. In fact, the faster I make the decision, the less likely I am to have truly made the decision. If an observer were to watch me make this decision in different settings, thousands of times, they would be able to predict with high accuracy what I would order at any given time.

But what if, on this particular day I decide that I would actually like to make a conscious decision about what I want to drink? Fortunately, we don’t have to answer the question about why that specific thought appeared in my mind, because it is not relevant to what I do next, or the narrowed scope in which we can practice individual choice. I have been primed to make a conscious decision, and now the focus is on what I do in that moment.

I scan across the menu again and weigh my options. What is a drink option that I have never tried before? I am intent on making a truly free choice, an unpredictable one. Wait, I realize, the probability of my choice has already been limited. I take for granted that the options on the menu are the only ones I can choose from. I should insist that the kitchen make me some new kind of drink with what they have on hand. Even then though, I am biologically limited by what humans deem drinkable. There are plenty of liquids that one could drink but which would taste awful (biologically incompatible with my inherited sense of taste) and at least a few liquids that taste terrific that are not compatible with continuing my life. Wait though, many liquids taste terrible when first consumed but then become enjoyable. I shouldn’t limit myself.

At this point the cost of making a truly free decision is beginning to get out of hand. Both the waiter and my friend are looking at me impatiently, and if I let the social pressure defeat me I will order the first thing my eye sees on the menu, spoiling my free will experiment. I buy more time by saying I still need a moment.

It is now becoming apparent to me that my ability to make a completely free choice, for even a simple matter, is not easy. It is highly dependent on what drinks I have tried before and what human imagination has previously deemed drinkable. It is even more dependent on the options offered in a particular scenario. I feel that I am at an impasse, so I take the only way out that I can think of, something that is seemingly only available to conscious humans: I reflect on every drink I can remember having and endeavor that the next liquid I consume will be entirely unique from anything I have ever had before. And this is the key, I believe, to our limited practice of free will. We are not entirely dependent on external stimuli to change our perception of something or recall experience to our mind. Calling all of this to my mind, I can think about many of the drinks I’ve had and reflect on the originality of each. Were I to order a drink one minute after beginning this reflection, it would be different than the drink ordered after two hours of deliberation. I do maintain, though, that I am free to make any drink choice I wish, based on a prolonged meditation. It is, however, exceedingly unlikely that I will make an original drink choice or that my imagination will escape the boundaries from within what it thinks it can choose.

Returning to my friend at the café, I decide to state that I will not drink another drop of liquid until I can consume something completely and totally original. Familiar with my strangeness, the friend chuckles and we continue talking while he drinks his iced tea and eats a club sandwich.

After four and a half days of looking for an original drink, I have not found one and I am near death. Predetermined patterns in my central nervous system have been heavily bombarding my conscious mind, and it has taken a significant amount of willpower not to break down and drink something unoriginal.  The cost of exercising true free will has indeed become very high, biologically speaking. As the darkness closes in, I realize with utter shame that my actions over the past few days were inextricably linked to my drink decision four and a half days earlier. If I had exercised an additional action of free will, beyond purposefully walking backwards throughout my entire journey for a drink, perhaps I would not be dying now. I think to myself that death in pursuit of an action of free will is not the most ridiculous reason someone has died.

Though this scenario is outrageous, it demonstrates the difficulty and extreme cost of making an original decision that runs contrary to behavioral survival patterns or shared cultural, stereotyped responses. Thus far this is all more or less conjecture, but I find the neuroscientific questions it raises very interesting. If free will is viewed as a rarely exercised state in which momentary decision-making exhibits probabilistic bias away from the most likely response, perhaps we *can* exhibit minute, transitory moments of unpredictable decision making which can be termed free will. Those decisions, though, will inherently be bounded by context dependent possibilities in most cases and will come at significant biological or social (if different than biological) cost.

In any given situation in which a decision can possibly be made by a sentient human, there is a high probability that their choice follows a predictable pattern specific to them. That pattern is dependent on every prior interaction and the current, unconscious state of that person’s brain. The person does not have significant control over the outcome, and merely experiences the choice as of their own making.

My hypothesis, however, is that when attention is drawn to a choice making activity, and internal reflection occurs, that is to say non-external-stimuli-based reasoning and information processing, a person can make a free choice through great effort, though most of the time those choices will be both internally and externally logical within the constraints of life as we know it.
