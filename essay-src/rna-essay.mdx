Though that was probably the last course I'll take in grad school—maybe ever, officially—it was foundational to how I think about the world, especially science. I was especially struck by the simplicity by which the rules established by ribosomes dictate everything else in biology. I haven't been able to escape seeing how simple rules at key interfaces shape how ecosystems of all kinds unfold: science funding, politics, career choice, artificial intelligence. I took this course during what unexpectedly turned into a transitionary period of my academic path. I am glad I did, and thank you to everyone who was a part of discovering the magic that is RNA.*

## Introduction

The past century has produced incredible advances in our understanding of the brain. The nervous system must coordinate everything from continuous, nearly instantaneous sensory perception to long-term memory formation. Networks of transcriptionally unique neural and glial cell types possess an information processing and retention capacity without parallel. Despite our progress in knowing these cell types are important in normal brain function, we have remarkably little idea how they operate either alone or at scale. We are reasonably convinced, however, that the neuron is the primary functional unit through which neural computation occurs.

Still, there is no agreed-upon set of fundamental principles that can describe or predict neural activity. A large body of work has focused on synaptic remodeling, a protein-centric view of neural computation, as the main mechanism by which neurons learn associations with the physical world. Through this lens, it is the physical connection of synapses, the relative strength of each connection, and the summed weight of inputs to each cell that determine whether an action potential occurs. Action potential generation then determines whether the neuron can propagate its signal to its post-synaptic neighbors in turn. This model is an important step forward, and synaptic remodeling no doubt shapes neural activity, but it increasingly looks like an insufficient explanation of how neurons function. Synaptic remodeling is better understood as the resulting molecular state, the final output of other complex regulatory events that unfold across time and space in the neuron.

Here I want to explore the possibility that RNA plays a much more central role in neural computation than we have suspected. There are already scattered examples of how even small changes to the sequence or post-transcriptional state of an RNA transcript can dramatically change the behavior of a cell or an organism. I will walk through several of them and make a general case for what is likely a significant capacity of the transcriptome (the total collection of transcribed RNAs in a cell at any moment) to provide short- and long-term, context-dependent molecular memory. That memory is not necessarily genetically encoded, and it offers more flexible and localized regulation in cells like neurons that rely heavily on spatial localization. Appreciating how RNA can do this improves our understanding of basic neural systems, and it can give biological engineers ideas for co-opting these systems to build better tools: tools that causally link states of the transcriptome to changes in neural activity and to behavior.

## A time before brains

Life as we know it appears to have arisen at a time when RNA served both the storage and catalytic functions later optimized by DNA and proteins.[^gilbert] It is still unclear how long this took, how many times it happened, or how many viable competing paths could have resulted in life, but the formation of the ancient ribosome quickly extinguished any alternatives and ushered in the era of efficient molecular replication and selection in which we still live. When scientists first turned their attention to our much more mature version of life, we relegated RNA to an intermediary role within the Central Dogma.[^crick] In that view, RNA relays sequence information from the optimized information medium (DNA) to build optimized catalytic domains (proteins).

The discovery and characterization of the ribosome returned some appreciation to RNA and highlighted all of life's remarkable dependence on the ribosome and, thus, on RNA.[^wimberly] In fact, the success of cells seems more tied to the RNA-protein relationship than to the DNA-protein relationship: some cells, such as mature red blood cells, operate for hundreds of days with little or no intrinsic DNA. As far as we know, no cell type can operate without RNA, given its centrality to biological function since the beginning of organic life.[^joyce] Modern DNA serves a useful but limited role, providing stable copies of transcripts on demand as transcriptomic and proteomic activity dictates. Even after transcription, these default gene sequences are heavily processed, influenced, or decayed by the molecular context of the cell, producing functionally heterogeneous outcomes, as I'll discuss later. This places the ribosome at the absolute core of all biological function, as the final and most dramatic point of control between RNAs and the functionally active proteins they become.

It is worth going this far back because even the simplest nervous systems are relatively new biological inventions, having existed for less than an eighth of the total duration of life on Earth. Early neurons likely developed by adapting action-potential-like motility mechanisms used by single cells.[^arendt] So early information processing took the form of metabolite recognition, nutrient seeking, or pathogen defense, and it developed under molecular rules established much earlier in evolution. More advanced forms of computation emerged on top of this history-dependent path.

As complex organisms evolved, they needed to coordinate an ever larger number of increasingly specialized cells. They seem to have achieved this not by evolving more numerous and more capable proteins, but by expanding the non-coding regions of their genomes in step with the biological control their size and complexity required.[^hahn] A meta-analysis by Liu and colleagues showed that in prokaryotes protein-coding bases increase exponentially with genome size, but level off at simple multicellular eukaryotes such as slime molds (e.g., *Dictyostelium*), while in more complex organisms it is non-coding RNA that keeps scaling with genome size.[^liu] Not only is most (>70%) of these larger genomes transcribed, but the transcripts are highly cell-type-specific, with as little as 2% of transcribed non-coding RNA overlapping between distinct cell types.[^liu] It is reasonable to conclude that complexity in single-celled organisms developed by expanding into the space of possible biochemical reactions, performing new or more efficient processes, whereas this set of catalytic functions became saturated early in eukaryotic evolution. Additional complexity then came from increasingly complex logic, embedded in non-coding regions, about when and where to deploy specific protein domains.

As multicellular organisms grew, so did the distance between communicating cells, their energy requirements, and the coordinated movement needed for sensory perception and planned action. Electrical signaling was critical here: it allowed rapid, targeted communication between cells that were not direct neighbors, solving a fundamental space (and thus computation) constraint of earlier organisms. The action potential, or spike, has become nearly synonymous with neural function because it is so obvious and dramatic, especially next to other cell types. It is not surprising that it has long dominated research into neural computation. And indeed, simple weighted connections of neurons have proven surprisingly capable of flexibly transforming a range of inputs into specified outputs. But across the full evolution of nervous systems, the action potential is a novel means of moving information between distant cells, not a fundamentally new form of information transfer. I would go further: the action potential is not the information being relayed but the means of relaying it. The rest of this essay outlines other forms of neural information processing, ones that account for what was truly new in what cells had to do to build working nervous systems.

## Neurons work across (relatively) vast scales of time and space

One unique feature of neurons is their ability to maintain sprawling, complicated morphologies and molecular architectures far from the main gene regulatory machinery in the nucleus. Branching dendritic trees and long projecting axons must operate semi-independently, far from the cell's genome, relying on a mix of diffusion and transport to traffic proteins, neurochemicals, and even RNA itself throughout the cell. This gets interesting when you consider the timescales over which neurons operate, which range from sub-milliseconds to decades (Figures 1a-d).

There is not enough time for single action potentials to relay information to and from the nucleus and every part of the neuron. Likewise, the synaptic connections involved in a memory must be maintained for decades despite ongoing action potentials in that same cell and the constant remodeling of dendritic spines. And how can spikes reliably lead to long-term remodeling when there is such a long delay before even the fastest immediate early genes (IEGs) become active (Figure 1c)? Neurons seem to continuously integrate information from the recent and distant past to output an updated protein state for future computations. Yet the mechanisms of molecular memory available to them operate on timescales either much shorter (protein activation and inactivation) or much longer (transcription, translation, and trafficking of new proteins across the neuron) than the evoked neural "code" itself (Figures 1a, d, e).

I propose that the complete state of the transcriptome, including base modifications and edits, post-transcriptional alterations, RNA-bound proteins, spatial localization, and structural arrangement, serves as a molecular memory. It extends how long a cell can internally "remember" a salient stimulus, which may itself last several seconds or minutes, up to the lifetime of the transcript. In this way neurons maintain continuity between proteomic and genomic states, while adding a more flexible and responsive layer of regulation directly on and within RNA. These are not passive accumulations on the transcript but heterogeneous processes with tangible functional consequences for the cell.

## Local ribosomes distribute regulatory control

Given how central ribosomes are to life, it is worth looking at the unusual demands neuronal ribosomes must meet under the constraints above. In a typical, less branched eukaryotic cell, precursors to the two ribosomal subunits are built in the nucleolus and exported to the cytoplasm to mature.[^bassler] Assembled ribosomes then spread roughly evenly through the cytoplasm to translate mRNA exported from the nucleus. When a peptide is bound for export or for a membrane, the ribosome and its protein localize to the rough endoplasmic reticulum, physically separating those peptides for further processing. This spatial localization matters: it stratifies function by compartmentalizing ribosomal output for specific purposes. Neurons push this compartmentalization much further by distributing ribosomes widely across dendrites, axons, and synaptic terminals.[^dastidar] The result is highly local translational environments that can vary dramatically across a single cell.

Recent work by Fusco and colleagues has also shown that ribosomes are much more dynamic than we thought.[^fusco] Ribosomes have traditionally been treated as functionally homogeneous, relying on signals on the RNA itself to bind and begin elongation. But the mRNA for many constitutive ribosomal proteins is itself trafficked to distal dendrites, where local ribosomes actively translate it. Those proteins that sit in solvent-accessible positions on the ribosome can then be assembled directly into existing ribosomes, extending their life far from the nucleus and changing their functional efficiency. This exchange of ribosomal proteins also appears specific to subcellular compartments and depends on the state of the cell.[^fusco] When cultured neurons were exposed to H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> to induce oxidative stress, and so increase the need for ribosomal repair, a number of ribosomal proteins were indeed assembled into dendritic ribosomes at a significantly higher rate.

Whether this kind of environmental recognition and response counts as information processing in the strictest sense is up for debate. What is clear is that the molecular interactions that once made up life's full arsenal for sensing and responding to the physical environment are still relevant in modern biology. It is also clear that these interactions, and the distributed nature of ribosomes in the neuron, set up a new set of principles from which much of the neuron's complex behavior arises. In this view, much of the regulatory control usually reserved for nuclear signaling, which ends in a gene being turned up or down, is handed to local collections of ribosomes and distally trafficked RNA. That gives more immediate and finer control over which proteins are translated, and where and when.

This shift to distributed processing opens up possibilities that were not available when regulation was centrally controlled. For one, it makes the large branching structures unique to neurons possible by solving the transport and delay problem. New transcripts still have to be made in the nucleus, but local compartments can maintain themselves and respond to events either by modulating their ribosomes directly, as above, or by acting directly on RNA, which I turn to next. Another consequence is that it vastly expands the number of distinct molecular processing sites in a network of connected cells. If neuronal regulation were strictly central, the number of computational nodes in a network would equal the number of cells. With distributed molecular processing, the number of nodes grows dramatically, and a single neuron can integrate more information, and hold it longer, than a single action potential lasts.

## Neural computation as an RNA-centric process

So far I have focused on the ribosome itself: distributed through the neuron's compartments, and repaired and modulated both alongside and apart from central regulation. Now I want to turn to features and modifications on mRNA itself that directly affect physiology. This small set of examples is meant to show an underlying logic by which RNA can dynamically shape how and when a cell behaves in a given context. It moves RNA from the passive role of relaying a message to an active one, in which part of the RNA sequence is itself a translator and an effector, deploying proteins with a specific function at a specific place and time.

### RNA-guided editing and protein recoding

Double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) is classically associated with viral infection, and it typically triggers immune pathways in vertebrate cells.[^chen] A family of adenosine deaminases that act on dsRNA (ADARs) somewhat paradoxically dampens the host response to viral dsRNA by converting adenosines to inosines (A-to-I) fairly nonspecifically, destabilizing secondary structure. ADAR seems to play another major role in mammalian cells: diversifying the proteome through edits that are not encoded in the genome and that use wobble base-pairing at the ribosome to recode proteins.[^yang] In cells such as neurons, which rely on many selective ligand recognition sites to trigger specific signaling cascades and outputs, changing even a single amino acid can matter. This has indeed been shown for ADAR-targeted substrates, in both incremental and all-or-nothing ways. Two of the classic examples involve specific glutamate (GluR) and serotonin (5-HTR) receptors. The first is a case of obligate editing, needed to make a Q/R (glutamine-to-arginine) swap in the pre-mRNA for GluR-B, an ionotropic receptor that mediates fast excitatory neurotransmission. The second shows edits accumulating incrementally and steadily reducing the 5-HT<sub>2C</sub>R protein's affinity for serotonin.

Several ionotropic GluRs undergo A-to-I editing, but only one position, on GluR-B, is edited 100% of the time.[^higuchi] When ADAR2-deficient mice cannot make this edit, GluR-B pre-mRNA builds up and mature GluR-B mRNA is depleted throughout the brain. The result is lethal: excitotoxicity and early-onset epilepsy.[^brusa][^feldmeyer] The phenotype is partly rescued by one copy of ADAR2 and fully rescued by restoring the Q/R substitution in GluR-B. This is a striking case of a single amino acid change, not encoded in the genome, with dramatic consequences for the whole organism. It is not clear what advantage this could have over simply encoding it in the genome, or whether it is an evolutionary quirk of A-to-I editing returning a protein to its original state.

In contrast to the edit GluR-B requires before it works, 5-HT<sub>2C</sub>R starts out functional when unedited and then accumulates edits at several sites with varying efficiency. Unedited, amino acids 156–160 give an isoleucine-asparagine-isoleucine (INI) configuration. Successful A-to-I edits at all five positions convert this to valine-glycine-valine (VGV). As edits accumulate in the 5-HT<sub>2C</sub>R mRNA, and so in the translated proteins, serotonin becomes less able to activate the receptor, least of all in the fully edited form.[^niswender] More recent work by Warhaftig and colleagues showed that editing of 5-HT<sub>2C</sub>R can have direct and lasting behavioral consequences.[^warhaftig] This example is particularly pointed given how much we rely on sequencing and counting to study the transcriptome. Here, up to 32 different mRNA versions can produce 24 functionally distinct protein isoforms without any change in overall mRNA levels. Methods that do not explicitly measure individual base changes are completely blind to these consequences.

Both examples show context-dependent changes to transcripts that meaningfully change the proteome. We need better methods to survey the whole transcriptome for functionally active A-to-I sites, and for the mechanisms cells use to control them. Still, the possibilities are exciting. This is also an example of a potentially RNA-guided control mechanism, in which complementary regions of RNA recruit A-to-I editing in either cis or trans. It is unclear whether a second substrate is needed to guide the edits, and whether that guide might be part of another regulatory element in the transcriptome. RNA-directed RNA modification opens an entire theoretical landscape for control and regulation, possibly within the local compartments discussed above, since there is some evidence of A-to-I editing in distal regions of the neuron.[^vallecillo]

### RNA structure as a functional unit

Moving beyond sequence alone, the transcriptome is rich in secondary, tertiary, and even quaternary structure that is hard to fully appreciate. The mechanisms governing RNA secondary structure are difficult to isolate, partly because the transcriptome is so dynamic, but it is now well established that the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of mRNAs confer specific regulatory and localization functions on the same strand (in cis). One important secondary structure, found in guanine-rich DNA and RNA, is the G-quadruplex (G4): stacks of guanine tetrads held together by non-canonical Hoogsteen hydrogen bonds and by monovalent cations such as K<sup>+</sup>. G4s are among the most stable secondary structures in RNA. In the 5′ UTR they can either impede or promote translation, depending on how they interact with the ribosome; in the 3′ UTR they can recruit other regulatory proteins.

One of the clearest examples of what such regions do is in the 5′ UTR of the *Task3* mRNA, which codes for a two-pore, acid-sensitive K<sup>+</sup> leak channel that sets the resting membrane potential in many neuron types. *Task3* expression shifts the resting potential, which can in turn affect action potential initiation, kinetics, and recovery. The *Task3* 5′ UTR has a (GGN)<sub>13</sub> repeat between the 5′ cap and the translation start site. Maltby and colleagues probed its function by adding a 3xFLAG tag at the 3′ end of the coding region, letting them see the translated protein under a range of conditions.[^maltby] An intact G4 sequence produced the least protein of any condition. Whole-cell patch clamp recordings by the same authors showed that adding the G4-specific helicase DHX36 increased translation of the channel, increasing K<sup>+</sup> efflux and hyperpolarizing the membrane. The *Task3* 5′ UTR G4 is not only important for normal physiology; it also directs transport of the mRNA away from the soma.

We are only beginning to be able to study secondary structure in vivo while directly measuring a neural or behavioral phenotype. The *Task3* example gives some idea of what might be possible, though. The structure that emerges within the transcript protects it from spurious translation where it isn't needed and helps carry it to distributed compartments throughout the neuron, where local ribosomes can make the protein when it is needed. Action potentials and other ion movement across local membranes, including K<sup>+</sup> efflux during repolarization, could plausibly destabilize structures such as G4s, gating cycles of translation in step with neural activity. That would let the local molecular environment anticipate upcoming physiological demands, with the logic built directly into the structure of the mRNA. On its face, this looks like at least a partial solution to the spatial and temporal regulation problems unique to neurons.

### Pathway-dependent isoform splicing

Alternative splicing is highly prevalent in the brain: more than 90% of multi-exon protein-coding genes in humans undergo some form of it.[^mazille] This form of post-transcriptional control is widely conserved and shapes tissue- and cell-type-specific gene expression across the genome. Splicing is also an important way cells control subcellular localization and swap in the protein domains a finished transcript needs. One way cells achieve this localization is targeted intron retention, which holds pre-mRNAs in the nucleus. Immature RNAs stay physically separated from ribosomes until a specific cue arrives from elsewhere in the cell.

Mazille and colleagues showed that distinct signaling pathways act differently on subpopulations of these nuclear transcripts, sending them either to degradation or to targeted splicing and export.[^mazille] They stimulated cultured neurons either with brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) or by blocking GABA<sub>A</sub> receptors with bicuculline. Both robustly increased neural activity, and, even with transcription blocked, each kind of stimulation produced its own pattern of up- and down-regulation of intron-retaining transcripts in the nucleus.

This last example shows RNA programmability responding to the environment independently of the genomic sequence. Keeping a pool of long, unprocessed, pre-transcribed RNAs isolated in the nucleus helps solve one of the timing problems from earlier. Rather than answering every external signal with a full cascade (signaling, then new transcription, export, trafficking, and finally translation), neurons prepare their options ahead of time and then process and release the right set of RNAs in response to cues carried by specific signaling pathways. This may even be part of why cell type diversity has come to dominate the architecture of the brain. It is more favorable for a given cell type to predefine its possible transcriptomic actions from a small, cell-type-specific set, which can then be deployed quickly and reliably in the larger network.

## Conclusion

Pulling these threads together, RNA appears to play not just a major but an irreplaceable role in the neuron's ability to process, store, and transmit information across such dramatically different timescales. In the prototype model I've laid out (Figure 2), the most important functional unit of the neuron is the compartmentalized ribosome, distributed widely across each cell's dendritic spines and within individual dendritic boutons. Each ribosome "neighborhood" draws on the mRNAs present locally, some of which can regulate for themselves the conditions under which they are translated. As much information processing as can be done locally and immediately is done that way. RNAs in these local environments may carry a variable-length "memory," in which accumulated modifications recode the proteome at that specific location. Through this mechanism, the epigenetic "memory" a transcript accumulates over its life matches the RNA to the needs of its location and its moment.

When the combined local electrical activity reaches a threshold, a full action potential is generated, and it serves two purposes. First, it enables long-range communication by releasing neurochemicals at a distant site (the projecting synapse), passing molecular context to a wider network of local ribosome neighborhoods. Second, it signals back within the cell which pre-transcribed RNAs need to be replenished. Those possible RNA outputs are limited in a cell-type-specific way, and they are selected not only by which combinations of active protein domains the cell needs, but also by the non-coding RNA context in which those domains are embedded. That context carries pre-packaged instructions about when, where, and to what end a protein should be translated, while these molecules operate far from the nucleus.
